On the ontological limitations of digital twins

Artemio S. Arcega III
Department of Philosophy, College of Social Sciences and Philosophy, University of the Philippines – Diliman
Presented at Analysis: The Emmanuel Q. Fernando Philosophy Undergraduate Conference, April 2026

As modern computing moves toward high-fidelity modelling of complex physical phenomena, a philosophical tension emerges between the independent digital representation and the target systems of reality it claims to mirror. Using Landauer's claim that information is physical, and the ontological analytical framework of Gehlert and Esswein, I interrogate the status of equivalence through physical law and mathematical isomorphism.

The question is whether a target system — an ontology, in this case the continuum that is reality — can ever be represented equivalently in a digital substrate through a digital twin. There exists a fundamental limitation of digital substrates in their discreteness, which posits a construct deficit and excess, and incompleteness by the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen criterion. So for all cases, there exists a divergence in the ontological equivalence of a target system and its digital representation.

I prove this five ways: by cardinality, by thermodynamics, by quantum mechanics, by mappings, and by signals. The conclusion is that regardless of increased fidelity, there exists a mathematical, physical and ontological limitation for equivalence that is contradictory to the laws of nature.

Keywords — digital twins, modeling, information theory, ontology, systems

1Introduction

A digital twin (DT) is a mirror image of a target physical process or system that is articulated. As computing moves toward high-fidelity modelling — the push for artificial general intelligence, world models, extensive mappings of the physical world for predictive capacity — a philosophical question emerges. Can digital twins, and every technology under that category, ever reach a fidelity that simulates real-time phenomena to absolute accuracy with no ontological residue, and perhaps even predict future events within the system it represents?

Batty (2018) states that a target system and its DT cannot be equivalent, because at equivalence they become merged and identical, which contradicts the DT's existence as a twin and collapses it. In other words, the DT becomes an identity of the target system. Tomko and Winter (2019) claim Batty's argument is "plagued by a variety of misinterpretations" — arguing for its relevance where Batty initially claims irrelevance, only to state relevance by the end of his work.

Here, instead of debating those matters, I investigate the ontology itself, and its equivalence to the physical realities DTs attempt to capture, predict and are trained on. By Landauer (1991)'s claim that information is physical, I use physical law and mathematical logic to prove the non-equivalence of DTs and their target system, within the framework of ontological analysis provided by Gehlert and Esswein.

The scope focuses on global and local systems without targeting any system by choice. I will not be delving into the intricacies of artificial neural networks themselves, as that would reach into epistemological discussion and present a stacked overflow of philosophical problems. DTs here are inclusive of their subcategories: CAD modelling, artificial intelligence, and other simulations of physical phenomena that can be measured empirically, exclusive of the human aspect of information, in a perspective similar to Shannon's.

This paper aims to achieve two things:

  1. An improved understanding of the fundamental limitations of computational modelling with discrete, silicon-based computers, even at hypothetically highest fidelity.
  2. A concise, physically-grounded means of ontologically analysing artificial intelligence, digital twins and computational models.

Eight axioms carry the whole argument. Each proof in section 5 draws on a different subset of them, which is what makes the result robust: no single axiom, if contested, takes down every proof at once. The dependency matrix in Figure 2 makes that structure explicit.

4Criteria for ontological equivalence

The formalization of March and Allen (2007)'s Ontological Foundations for Active Information Systems by Gehlert and Esswein (2007) is one of the foundational frameworks used here. They formalize ontological analysis mathematically and define four requirements used both for evaluating ontological frameworks and ontology-in-itself. I use their requirements in the evaluation of ontological equivalence, plus one additional criterion which may also be considered under the requirements: the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen paradox.

I use select terminologies interchangeably, particularly the modeling grammar, which may also be theory, in the context of EPR the digital twin itself, the digital substrate, and symbolically, most commonly, D, wherein D contains elements of a digital substrate d.

Ontology, as used in the framework, is what I use as the target system or the reality itself. Note that target systems are not always reality, but I use reality as the target system here, as measurements must be made in reality to be twinned.

The four mapping pathologies

A construct deficit refers to a lack in the representation of the modeling grammar D to the ontology R. A construct excess means there is excess information or element in D that is lacking in R. An ontological overload is where an element of D is mapped to represent more than one element of R. Construct redundancy is the converse: several elements of D standing for one element of R.

The EPR criterion for completeness

If a physical element r of the physical reality R can be predicted with certainty in D, without a disturbance in the system, there must exist a counterpart in the physical theory, which in this case is our modeling grammar D (Einstein et al., 1935). Denoted:

$$comp(D, R) \equiv \forall r \in R,\ \exists d \in D$$

such that $f(d) = r$ for a digital substrate to be ontologically equivalent to its target system.

5Proofs

Five independent routes to the same conclusion. Each one invokes a different subset of the axioms, and each lands on a status: either sim(D, R) — the mapping is merely similar, not equivalent — or Δ(D, R), where the two are outright separate physical entities.

5.1 — Proof by cardinality

Writing in terms of bijective mapping:

$$equiv(D, R) \supset f : |R| \xrightarrow{\sim} |D|$$

By Axiom 2, R is a continuous manifold in real-valued fields, in set theory of cardinality $|R| = 2^{\aleph_0}$. By Axiom 6, the digital substrate is limited to discrete operations, thus countably infinite, $|D| = \aleph_0$. The criterion wants all elements $r \in R$ mapped by a value $d \in D$ regardless of whether the system is locally or globally defined over its domain.

It is mathematically impossible for a discrete set to be bijectively mapped onto a continuous set and vice versa, due to the difference in cardinality. Therefore a contradiction and a collapse emerges from the conditions of completeness and equivalence, as there exist construct deficits:

$$1 :\ \sim ( f : |R| \xrightarrow{\sim} |D| )$$ $$2 :\ equiv(D,R) \supset ( f : |R| \xrightarrow{\sim} |D| = \forall r \in R, \exists d \in D )$$ $$\therefore\ \sim equiv(D, R) \quad \text{(2,1 MT)}$$

There is an infinite construct deficit: uncountably many points in the continuum R are unaccounted for in D. Recall that states in R must be accounted for in D. Therefore the mapping is only similar, by requirement 3.

5.2 — Proof by thermodynamics

By Landauer (1991)'s principle, any increase in the resolution of D with respect to R leads to a proportional increase in entropy, $\Delta S > 0$. The erasure of one bit posits an exact change of $k_B \ln 2$.

As we increase the resolution and complexity of D, the information that must be processed increases as we decrease δ. By Axiom 1, the work required for the digital substrate to update its state is limited to the erasure of its bits, because it cannot store infinite information. By Axiom 4, there always exists a continuous change in the states as the model updates and erases the previous ones.

Since R is a continuous manifold by Axiom 2, its state contains infinite information. For D to achieve equivalence there must be an infinite erasure rate to keep up with the continuity of R. To achieve it identically, with δ = 0, there would be an infinite number of bit erasures and updates within every infinitesimal moment. The total Landauer cost therefore approaches infinity as the fidelity of the twin approaches the continuous limit of reality, $\Delta t \to 0$:

$$L = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \sum \frac{k_B T \ln 2}{\Delta t} \qquad \therefore\ L \to \infty$$

By Axiom 1, a physical substrate cannot support a complete digital twin without violating the Second Law of Thermodynamics. By the framework, there must exist a construct residue that is unaccounted for if we assume a global system.

5.3 — Proof by quantum mechanics

Here I use Wootters and Zurek (1982)'s No-Cloning Theorem, which posits that it is impossible to create an identical, independent copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state. Define ℚ as the quantum state of an element $r \in R$, and ℂ as the boolean possibility of cloning it. Recall that equivalence must mean that elements, including quantum states and information, are equivalent:

$$equiv(D,R) \equiv \forall r \in R, \exists d \in D \mid |\mathbb{Q}_d\rangle = |\mathbb{Q}_r\rangle$$

Let $s_D$ represent the blank slate of the digital substrate before initialization and $U$ the unitary twinning operator. Formally, no unitary operator $U$ can copy an arbitrary quantum state. If ℚ is a superposition, then any attempt to copy a state results in collapse:

$$\nexists U \mid U(\mathbb{Q}_R \otimes s_D) = \mathbb{Q}_R \otimes \mathbb{Q}_D$$

Any copy equated from the unitary operator in D would be a classical, reduced approximation — a measurement outcome $Q_D$ — with an unlike quantum state to the real system $\mathbb{Q}_R$. Such measurements are necessary for either initialization or assimilation by Axiom 4. If the two are inequivalent by the act of measurement, there exists a divergence δ ≠ 0.

If we attempt to force a duplication of states, R and D become entangled — made inseparable. Entanglement, where measurement on one subsystem constrains the outcomes of another, posits that the two are no longer independent entities. Therefore, to be a twin, $D = R$. But by Axiom 8, $D \neq R$. This violates Axiom 8. This is the one proof whose status is Δ(D, R) rather than similarity: the two are not merely approximate, they are separate physical entities.

5.4 — Proof by mappings

Here I focus on a much smaller system, looking at intervals rather than global sets. By Axioms 2 and 6, the shift from a continuum to a discrete substrate fundamentally collapses the causal continuity of reality.

In a continuous manifold R, for any two points $r_1, r_2$ there exists an infinite number of intermediate states in their interval $I(r_1, r_2)$. By Axiom 6 there must exist a minimum distance to move from $r_1$ to $r_2$ in intervals. Thus an ontological residue emerges in δ > 0. In a way, this perspective may be likened to Zeno's Paradox, in reality and in theory.

Physical laws in R are defined by equations that use approximations of infinity and zero. Discrete representations cannot do such a thing. Take a Riemann sum and the formal integral: there always exist intervals in the space. By Axiom 3, each block is a physical state. Applying Landauer's principle, placing each hypothetical block to approximate a hill's area would require infinite energy, and infinite heat — which by the proof of thermodynamics violates the Second Law, and therefore Axiom 1.

5.5 — Proof by signals

The Nyquist-Shannon theorem (Nyquist, 1928; Shannon, 1949) posits that a continuous time signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if the sampling rate $f_s$ is greater than twice the highest frequency component.

In a continuous system R, there are fluctuations at arbitrarily small scales. If R possesses infinite bandwidth, then the highest frequency approaches infinity as well. A requisite to equivalence would be an infinite sampling rate, necessitating infinite memory and processing power, which conclusively violates Axiom 3 and thermodynamics.

6Conclusion

The representation mapping of D will always be ontologically barred from equivalence with R, incomplete by the EPR, and similar by the framework. As fidelity increases, there exists a fundamental physical limitation for ontological equivalence by construct excess, and a mathematical limitation of construct deficit. Therefore there exists information noise generated by complexity scaling that contributes to the physical excess.

7Supplementary cases

7.1 — The argument of a binary universe

There is a reasonable dilemma when a fundamental axiom is challenged. Suppose Wheeler (1989)'s postulate of It-from-Bit is correct, so R is not a continuous manifold. In this case we operate in a world where Axiom 2 is discarded: reality is fundamentally discrete and informational, and the fundamental stuff is information, emerging from binary units. The universe would then be an exponentially complex binary composite of operations — in which the proofs by cardinality and mappings fall short.

If R is already digital, then a digital substrate of it could theoretically achieve ontological equivalence and completeness by mapping bijectively. However, for D to be a twin, it must exist on a substrate within R independently. If the universe were a computer, then D is a simulation within that computer, sort of like the philosophical equivalent of a virtual machine. To represent its target system perfectly, it would need more bits than the target system itself, by Bekenstein.

7.2 — Floridi's levels of abstraction

I had initially considered Floridi (2010)'s Levels of Abstraction as a fundamental axiom, but that in itself would create an epistemological leap of sorts. All possible outcomes of twinning would posit an abstraction. When a proponent claims they have achieved formal isomorphism and ontological equivalence, they are simply making a claim about an abstraction of fundamental reality that is uninterpretable. In addition, such a claim would in fact be practically impossible, as there emerges an immutable perspectival boundary between an observer and the natural world.

7.3 — Turing's argument from continuity of the nervous system

In Computing Machinery and Intelligence, one of the objections to the question 'Can Machines Think' by Turing (1950) was the continuous nature of the nervous system as an analog system, against the possibility of a discrete machine — both the analog computer of his time and the computer of now — ever being able to think.

In a way, and largely in fact, this is very similar to the central proofs presented in this paper. However, it is important to acknowledge that this rests under the fundamental assumption of the continuity of physical reality, which up to now is being debated in fundamental physics — a debate that I hope this paper will at the very least contribute to, ever so slightly.

8Discussion

8.1 — On world models and functional equivalence

As we conclude that ontological equivalence is physically impossible and fundamentally limited for digital twins, the contemporary push for world models as a prospect in modern AI remains trivial in its pursuit of accuracy. However, as we also conclude, it is not necessarily a limitation for functional equivalence. In fact, they may be considered similar.

It has become apparent that the prospect of digital twinning is widely what the industry wishes to pursue as the next step in this technology. However it must come to their realization that such models may never achieve the absolute minimum of error. It is, however, not really the goal to reach such a threshold of error.

Still, I argue that even as we increase epochs, nodes, layers and data centres, we may never reach the fidelity of the continuum of the universe. It remains unclear whether AGI, world models, or actual copied neurons are actual intelligence, actual "realities", and are actual living beings. It is only clear to us that they will never become of the same quality as what is ontological.

8.2 — On Wheeler's It-from-Bit

As with section 7.1, there may exist a challenging outcome with the acceptance of a digital reality. However, by Landauer's physicality, although information is physical, a binary unit is not necessarily the fundamental stuff. Instead it exists as a separate physical manifestation that generates entropy. A perfect "copy" of such a binary world would need more bits than the target system contains.

Reality cannot be information alone, and it cannot be the fundamental stuff, as there remains a structure of reality that is unmapped by any hypothetical digital representation, as they are both mathematically and physically constrained.

8.3 — On simulation theory

In the possibility that our lived system of reality is not necessarily what our target reality is, and we are incomprehensibly complex simulacra in an incomprehensibly complex simulation, then there exists a reality of a magnitude higher than us that is the true target reality, and we are an imperfect imitation in a substrate.

There are two possibilities here. If we are living in a digital substrate in highly complex hypothetical computers, then we are fundamentally barred from being real. If we are living in a hypothetical continuum — a hypothetical, infinitely complex analog computer — then we may be in a reality that is not fundamentally barred, and there may exist worlds like ours in the same fidelity, and we may even be of the same fidelity as the precedence of our world.

8.4 — On temporality

It is important to consider the question of time. Although we can model local systems fairly easily — so much so as a feat of the ingenuity of physics and engineering, where we may model aerodynamics, thermodynamics, chemical reactions and so on — a global model, or even a model that just accounts for more than one physical object at a time, its interactions and its dynamics, remains significantly difficult to both maintain and create.

Artificial neural networks, with the likes of physics-informed neural networks, have managed to solve some of these problems, as with AlphaFold where millions of proteins were finally given 3D molecular structures, which would have taken centuries to accomplish with manual work.

So then, will time-series models be able to account for such a vast amount of calculation and information processing as the real world? To this, I say no. It is important to consider the finiteness of both resources and of the universe, as shown with thermodynamic limits in section 5.2. Hypothetically, should a digital twin be able to become independent and equivalent, only then will it become able to predict everything. As we prove that it is a fundamental limitation, exact phenomena of both global and local systems may never be convergent. At a certain point in time, there will always be divergence. In a global model of a target system, from its inception, it will always be divergent.
