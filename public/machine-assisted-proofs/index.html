Machine-assisted proofs & syntactic manipulation

Artemio S. Arcega III
Department of Philosophy, College of Social Sciences and Philosophy, University of the Philippines – Diliman
Submitted for PHILO 197, Philosophic Questions · May 2026

The Erdős problems are a set of open combinatorial and number theoretical conjectures that require proofs demanding extensive mathematical ability, expertise, and most especially a creative intuition. Recently it was remarkably shown that one of these problems — #728 — was solved machine-autonomously with a combination of two deep learning models, Barretto and GPT-5.2, without reproduction of methods from any known, existing literature.

This is an extreme case of what is called a machine-assisted proof, in which machines autonomously solve such problems. It became an interesting prospect that with these novel instances, I am able to argue against the Chinese Room argument, in which such outputs would be merely 'clever syntactic manipulation'.

It may be argued that it is made even more likely due to the nature of proof-solving, literature, and the tools used to verify it; pertaining to the format of literature in LaTeX, proof assistance languages like Lean. So in this case it is logically valid to say that it is all syntax, no semantics, and hence a set of meaningless predicted symbols. However, as its training set is closed — meaning it contains only syntax involved in propositional logic, symbolic proofs, and mathematical deduction — I show why it is unlikely the case that such lone syntactic manipulation is the correct interpretation of the origin of such proofs, and that the novel autonomous proof showcases the case that we are seeing the rise of Strong AI.

Keywords — philosophy of AI, Chinese Room argument, deep learning, philosophy of mind and language

1Introduction & framework

1.1 — What is AI?

When I pertain to artificial intelligence, I am pertaining not exclusively to a large language model, but extending to its other siblings — extensively, but not exclusively, the likes of deep learning algorithms like AlphaFold, GNoME, NVIDIA Modulus. A majority of the conceptual formations in this paper come from Yingjin's lectures compiled in Fifteen Lectures on Artificial Intelligence Philosophy (2026).

Although it is not the case that we use the LLM as the strict definition for what AI is, it is important to realize that most of the processes involved in all sorts of such remain, and are by nature, similar in its use of symbols — and hence, syntax.

1.2 — Searle's concept of strong and weak AI

In Minds, Brains and Programs (1984), Searle creates a clear distinction between the concepts Strong and Weak AI. Strong AI is denoted as a computer properly programmed, a mind. Weak AI is, at most, a tool used to investigate an abstraction of activities of the mind.

The Chinese Room illustrates the following: a native speaker of English is locked in a room, communicating with people outside only by passing notes under the door. The paper notes can only be written in Chinese; the test subject is unable to semantically nor syntactically comprehend the Chinese language, and the examiner does not know whether the test subject understands it. Suppose also that the test subject is able to carry out specific instructions from a manual written in English, but does not know the semantics of the Chinese symbols he slides.

In the case that he passes the language test — that is to say, the examiner cannot tell the difference between the syntactic and the semantic understanding of the test subject — then likewise with the Turing test and the modern LLM, in hindsight it seems to be the case that it is just mere syntactic manipulation at hand here.

That is to say, Yingjin criticizes this notion as modern developments have already far surpassed such simple games of semantics. As he puts it, "if we suppose that such a machine is already created, can we genuinely say that it has a mind?". I, for one, think this is a reasonable place to start disassembling this initial notion of mere syntactic manipulation.

1.3 — Tao's concept of machine-assisted proofs

Tao's paper for the American Mathematical Society, Machine Assisted Proof (2025), discusses the contemporary development, the history, and the general definition of what a machine-assisted proof is. In its essence, it is a combination of machine learning algorithms and proof assistants. Recent history, as Tao discusses, shows that such assistants are not ineffable, but remain widely used, and even a standard for the state of the field of mathematics and logic.

The basic operation of using proof assistants and AI for developing, discovering and creating proofs is, in its simplest, the use of the syntax of mathematical language — a higher-order abstraction of the binary logic in the computer, as a programming language of sorts. Basic rules of the axiomatic system are defined and hence are able to verify the validity of such proofs for describing a specific theorem or conjecture.

Suppose then that we utilize a proof assistant for proving any problem from a given ballot of open mathematical problems. We are given its full description, its axiomatic system. The reasonable case is for a mathematician, or at least a well-adept person fluent in mathematical language, to use proof assistants in tandem with their rationality and intuition. However, there exists a case emerging as a consequence of modern AI prospects, such as the transformer and the integration of big data, that there emerge autonomous proofs.

2Problem #728

Prior to our discussion, although not essential to its essence, the Erdős problems are a set of open problems and conjectures developed by mathematician Paul Erdős et al. Such problems span the areas of number theory and combinatorics.

For any ε > 0 and sufficiently large n0, there exist infinitely many integers n ≥ n0 such that φ(n) < nε and φ(n+1) < (n+1)ε.

Originally posed by Erdős, Graham, Ruzsa and Straus, the problem builds upon Erdős's prior proof that φ(n) < nε holds for ε < 1. While the initial formulation allowed for trivial solutions, such as those involving divisors, analysis suggests the condition was intended to imply ε → 0. Recent progress by Barretto and ChatGPT-5.2 confirms this interpretation, successfully proving that for any ε, there are infinitely many such integers n satisfying the constraints.

So then, what do we make of such?

3Against claims of syntactic manipulation

As simple as such a proof may seem, the reason why this case became a point of interest is because it was solved purely autonomously by a proof assistant and an OpenAI model. Consider how such things work: when we talk about the state of artificial intelligence, we consider only syntactic manipulation, and the stochastic parrot concept. That is, the only thing such models output are what is fed, processed, controlled, condensed. In other words, particularly through the lens of Searle, we only consider that what is illustrated is that it is a Weak AI.

The problem emerges when we consider its origin. We first reasonably infer that creating proofs would require a mathematician — in other words, an actual, general intelligence enacting upon a tool, which we claim to be Weak AI. However, since it is the case that such were solved autonomously, by a person with no postdoctorate nor mathematical background at that, not even as extremely defined as autodidacts like Ramanujan, the problem was solved through extensive "reasoning", or at least what is defined by the consensus as "reasoning" for most deep learning models as of late by companies like Google, OpenAI and Anthropic.

So what exactly is the nature and the origin of the proof? What was discovered, primarily, was a link between two fields of mathematics that was not considered — namely, the connection between discrete geometry and algebraic number theory. That is, although some patterns have been observed between the use of inter-area concept development in mathematics, such exact ideas to solve such a problem had not been done nor suggested yet, as a review of the existing literature on either area would say. And so, it is not merely a showcase of general intelligence that we see, as is described in Weak AI, but rather there must have also existed a certain semantic understanding of causal links between the two areas, and the nature of the problem itself that must have posited such a connection.

And so, such a capacity for general reasoning, and the linking of such areas, are not exclusive to biological intelligence, so it seems. Suppose the case that we are to develop a drug, but a solution is hidden within a myriad, a labyrinth of a library. We are unable to process each of these books to infer a reasonable link, and we are limited in the capacity of memory for such. I argue then that the link for such, and the discovery of such a link, is the necessary condition of the intelligent being — and hence such models do not only hold a capacity for syntactic links, but for semantic links as well, as the link itself can very well be established as a new unit of knowledge.

Consider the link between light, mass and energy, by Einstein. Such an idea is, arguably, not only a mere new concept or idea emerging from nothing, but an idea emerging from the existing that Einstein discovered a link to. That is, if such is the basis for extraordinary intelligence, then what is to say that a model discovering a link between fields and concepts is not?

Everything turns on which of two interpretations of the same event we accept. Both are consistent with the output; they differ on what produced it.

4On intentionality

Intentionality is, as it is commonly defined, the power of the mind to be about, or to stand for things, for states of affairs. It is not within the scope of mathematical language that we may define intentionality, and hence it is requiring of qualitative description. It is such a concept that we are concerned of when trying to evaluate the characteristic of an AI to be strong or to be weak. The challenge of intentionality stems from the disconnect of the model to meaningfully interpret natural language in the same way that we do when pertaining to both a thing, and the thing.

Suppose that we are pertaining to the Pokémon Reshiram. It is either the case that we are pertaining to my Reshiram, or some other person's Reshiram, or we are pertaining to Reshiram, in intentionality, as the Reshiram of the Pokémon canon — the concept of Reshiram as dictated by Nintendo.

When we create such an input to a model, we are unable to know whether the model truly understands which Reshiram we are pertaining to. That is to say, it is not a limitation of models alone. A human could also suffer the same problem, supposing that they are lacking of the necessary context, or unable to read the context, or are unknowing of the existence of what we are pertaining to at all.

Diverging from the necessary groundwork of this paper, we now introduce the use of AI in developing mathematical proofs. It is either the case that such use is merely syntactic manipulation of mathematical language, or that such use indicates that AI is understanding of the semantics, the nature, and the ideas of mathematics not merely as a language, but in either the Platonic or the descriptive sense.

Interestingly enough, it does not matter whether the interpretation of mathematical language and mathematical concepts is Platonic or a description of reality alone, as the mere process of understanding the intentionality behind mathematical knowledge is already an indication of Strong AI — regardless of whether such a definition is preferable or not. Hence, even if we consider language as simply and essentially a description of the reality instead of pertaining to actual states of affairs in reality, the general case of general intelligence is unaffected.

As we discussed earlier, we conclude that it is unimportant whether the mathematical language is descriptive or actual floating points, ideas in a state space in another dimension as Plato would presume. However, when we consider intentionality, we are unsure of whether the reasoning or the thinking [as operationally used in artificial intelligence research] is pertaining to either. I, for one, think that such consideration is important as to know whether the AI knows what it is describing. Although a link is established, and new knowledge, and therefore intelligence is showcased, it is not the case that we are thinking of the same mathematical objects or ideas as the model. And hence, if we [and the model] are not thinking of the same arbitrary or non-arbitrary objects, then what are we or the model thinking of?

On the other hand, and to play devil's advocate, this could very well simply be an anomalous case in which the patterns observed from a paper or a set of papers, as with the rational inference would be, is observed and used by the model to come up with a coincidentally correct, and valid proof. Hence, it is only a coincidence and consequence of parroting that came to such a conclusion, and that the AI could very well not possess intentionality, and not possess the intelligence we consider. This is, in part, due to the nature of modern AI technologies pushing not only for single-tokenization practice, but with more extensive developments in progressing the technologies for block tokenizations, embeddings and so on.

However, if such were the case, then it could very likely be that our thinking may not be that different from models after all.
