ML-based phytochemical analysis for multi-objective optimization with efficacy and toxicity metrics

Artemio S. Arcega III
UP Data Science Society Bootcamp, Data Science 102 · April 2026

This is a solo project on computational biology and frontier drug discovery, built on static docking and toxicity data. The ligands of choice are phytochemicals, also known as green chemicals: the plant compounds you already eat, in fruit, tea, whole grains and nuts.

I docked 33 of them against 90 protein targets spanning 35 biological hallmarks, which comes out to 7,619 scored ligand–pocket pairs. Every compound carries a binding affinity and a predicted toxicity score, so candidates can be ranked on both objectives at once instead of one at a time. Four compounds clear both medians with room to spare: rutin, (−)-epigallocatechin, α-amyrin and paeoniflorin.

This remains a relatively simple project. No artificial neural networks, no dynamics, which puts it well behind others in the same field. What I want to demonstrate through it is a gauge of the possibility: that in-silico drug discovery and pre-screening can be made far more accessible, for citizen scientists and researchers alike.

The notebook this came from is 37 pages of static figures. This version is the same analysis rebuilt so you can reach into it — hover any mark for the numbers behind it, re-sort the distributions, switch the axes, and open the data tables from the bar above.

1What was measured

The dataset took about two days to build. It is self-curated and computed from scratch — not taken from Kaggle or any other internet source. Five stages, all of them reproducible with public tools:

  1. Find the binding sites. This was done with P2Rank, an amazing tool for locating and ranking valid binding sites across proteins without having to hand-specify a docking box for every ligand.
  2. Dock everything. Each ligand goes into the top three pockets of every protein with AutoDock Vina 1.2.7, ten poses per pocket, with only the best pose carried forward.
  3. Predict toxicity and pharmacokinetics with ADMET-AI, roughly 40 endpoints per compound, rolled into a weighted composite score.
  4. Append the hallmark signatures, so results can be read as biology and not just as PDB entries.
  5. Analyse: exploratory statistics, frontier search, then supervised models.

Two numbers drive everything that follows.

  • best_affinity — the strongest Vina pose for a ligand–pocket pair, in kcal/mol. More negative means tighter binding.
  • Composite_Tox_Score — a weighted roll-up of around twenty ADMET toxicity endpoints. Lower means safer.

What I am after is a frontier: compounds that bind hard and score clean. Neither number on its own is a drug candidate, which is exactly why the two have to be read on one plane.

The scoring function

Vina's affinity score is an empirically weighted sum of five interaction terms with a torsional penalty:

$$E_{\text{vina}} = w_1\,\text{gauss}_1 + w_2\,\text{gauss}_2 + w_3\,\text{repulsion} + w_4\,\text{hydrophobic} + w_5\,\text{Hbond} + w_6 \cdot N_{\text{rot}}$$

where $N_{\text{rot}}$ is the number of rotatable bonds and the $w_i$ are empirically fitted weights. The final score approximates binding free energy in kcal/mol. Totally unnecessary to include here, but I love this formula, so here it is.

The other three — (−)-epigallocatechin (the green tea compound), α-amyrin and paeoniflorin (from peony) — clear both medians comfortably.

5What actually predicts affinity

If a cheap molecular descriptor predicted binding affinity, screening would be free. So the question worth asking is whether any of our foundational metrics have a significant relationship with the others — does something like lipophilicity, a subset of the physicochemical metrics, correlate meaningfully with binding affinity? This has been attempted before, by Parker et al. among others.

Here are eleven physicochemical and ADMET descriptors against the two scaled objectives.

This is the quiet finding of the whole project. logP, the descriptor most often assumed to track binding, sits at r = 0.02. Multi-parameter modelling is not a stylistic preference here — no single descriptor carries the signal, so screening on one of them would be screening on noise.

For reference, CYP3A4 is a crucial enzyme found mostly in the liver and small intestine, responsible for metabolising over half of all clinically used drugs. It is the major detoxification enzyme, breaking down foreign organic molecules, toxins and medications. Its activity is inhibited by, among other things, grapefruit juice.

6Where the models succeed, and where they do not

Two learning tasks were run on the same feature matrix: regress the binding affinity, and classify the biological hallmark. The results split so sharply that the gap between them is the result.

A clean-featured gradient boosting run on physicochemical and ADMET descriptors alone reaches R² = 0.188, and a refined random forest reaches 0.188 as well. That number, not the 0.999, is what 2D descriptors are actually worth for predicting binding affinity.

It confirms that simple 2D physicochemical and ADMET descriptors have limited predictive power here, and suggests that 3D structural features or more complex representations are necessary for a model that means anything.

The disparity between the two tasks is worth sitting with. Molecular properties directly dictate binding thermodynamics, so the regression at least has something to grip. But those same properties represent only a fraction of the complex features required to map a molecule onto a broad biological cancer hallmark, which is why the classifier struggles.

8Limits worth stating

  • Static docking only. No molecular dynamics, so no entropy, no solvent, no induced fit. Vina scores are a pre-screen, not binding free energies.
  • Toxicity is predicted, not measured. The composite score is a roll-up of model outputs. Its discrete banding is visible in Figure 1 as horizontal striping, and it is clearly not continuous enough to use as a prediction target on its own.
  • Hallmark labels are assignments. Each protein stands in for a process by editorial choice, so hallmark-level medians inherit that choice as much as they reflect chemistry.
  • One pose per pocket. Only the best of ten poses is carried forward, which discards the ensemble a dynamics run would use.

None of this sinks the pipeline. It sets what the pipeline is for: narrowing 2,970 ligand–target pairs down to a handful worth the expensive simulation. It is important to acknowledge that static analysis cannot fully gauge the potency of these chemicals in drug development, but it is a considerable step as a pre-screening process, before anything even more computationally expensive.

A plan for the future

It would be of interest in future iterations that I use neural networks and implement reinforcement learning for better predictions, as is the case with tools like ADMET-AI and ProTox-II, which is precisely what is used here.

As for the elephant in the room, and something I have yet to dwell on completely: molecular dynamics. A future DS-103 project, I presume.
